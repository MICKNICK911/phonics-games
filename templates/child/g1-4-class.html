Unit 1 · Classifying Living Things

Sub-strand: Living and Non-Living Things
B3.1.1.1.1

Learning Objectives

  • Identify and give examples of living and non-living things in my environment.
  • Describe the characteristics that all living things share.
  • Group living things into plants and animals.

Key Words

Living thing
Something that grows, moves, breathes, feeds and reproduces.
Non-living thing
Something that does not grow, breathe or reproduce by itself.
Characteristic
A special quality that helps us tell one thing from another.
Reproduce
To produce young ones or new plants.

1.1 Living and Non-Living Things Around Us

Look around your classroom, your home and your community. You will see many things — some are alive and some are not. A living thing carries out certain activities that keep it alive. A non-living thing does not carry out these activities on its own.

Examples of living things you may see in Ghana are the cocoa tree, the goat, the tilapia in a river, and of course, you. Examples of non-living things are a stone, a wooden stool, a bicycle and a cup of water.

1.2 Characteristics of Living Things

All living things — whether plants or animals — share seven important characteristics:

  1. Movement — animals walk, swim or fly; plants bend towards light.
  2. Respiration — taking in air to get energy.
  3. Nutrition — taking in food or nutrients.
  4. Growth — getting bigger over time.
  5. Reproduction — producing young ones.
  6. Excretion — removing waste from the body.
  7. Response to change — reacting to light, heat, touch or sound.
Example from Ghana: A cocoa seedling planted on a farm grows taller each year, produces flowers and pods (reproduction), and its leaves turn towards the sun (response). A stone in the same farm never does any of these things.

Think About It

Is a river a living thing? It moves and can grow bigger when it rains. Explain your answer using the seven characteristics you have learnt.

Try This

Walk around your school compound for ten minutes. Draw a table with two columns — Living and Non-living. List at least eight things you see in each column.

Unit Summary

Living things grow, move, breathe, feed, reproduce, excrete and respond to changes. Non-living things do not carry out these activities. Living things can be grouped into plants and animals.

Unit 2 · Uses of Everyday Materials

Sub-strand: Materials
B3.1.2.1.1 · B3.1.2.1.2

Learning Objectives

  • Identify common materials used in my home, school and community.
  • Describe why each material is suitable for a particular use.
  • Group materials by their properties: strength, flexibility and transparency.

Key Words

Material
Anything used to make an object.
Property
A quality of a material, such as hardness or transparency.
Suitable
Right or proper for a particular purpose.
Transparent
Allows light to pass through clearly.

2.1 Materials We Use Every Day

Everything around us is made from one material or another. In Ghana, some of the most common materials are wood, metal, clay, plastic, glass, leather and cotton. Each of these materials has properties that make it useful for certain objects.

2.2 Choosing the Right Material

  • Wood is strong and easy to shape — used for stools, doors and roofing frames.
  • Metal is strong and waterproof — used for roofing sheets, pots and farm tools.
  • Clay can withstand heat — used for pots, bowls and water coolers.
  • Plastic is light and waterproof — used for buckets, basins and bags.
  • Glass lets light pass through — used for windows, bottles and mirrors.
  • Cotton is soft and absorbs water — used for clothing like kente and school uniforms.
  • Leather is strong and flexible — used for sandals, bags and footballs.
Example from Ghana: A clay pot cools water naturally because clay is porous and allows slow evaporation. That is why many homes in the northern regions still use clay pots to keep drinking water cool.

Unit Summary

Everyday objects are made from materials such as wood, metal, clay, plastic, glass, cotton and leather. Each material is chosen for its special properties — strength, flexibility, transparency or resistance to heat.

Unit 3 · Mixtures and Separation

Sub-strand: Materials
B3.1.2.2.1

Learning Objectives

  • Explain what a mixture is and give everyday examples.
  • Describe simple ways of separating mixtures: picking, sieving, filtering and evaporation.
  • Choose the best method to separate a given mixture.

Key Words

Mixture
Two or more substances combined but not joined together.
Separate
To take apart the parts of a mixture.
Sieving
Using a sieve to separate large and small solid pieces.
Filtering
Using a filter to separate a solid from a liquid.
Evaporation
When a liquid changes to gas.

3.1 What is a Mixture?

A mixture is made when two or more substances are put together but do not join to form something new. You can usually see the different parts of a mixture and separate them again. Examples you know well include rice and stones, sand and water, salt and water, and groundnut and salt.

3.2 Ways of Separating Mixtures

  • Picking (hand-picking): used when the parts are big enough to pick out, e.g. stones from rice.
  • Sieving: used when solid pieces differ in size, e.g. separating gari from bigger lumps.
  • Filtering: used to separate a solid from a liquid, e.g. removing sand from water.
  • Evaporation: used to recover a solid dissolved in a liquid, e.g. getting salt from salt water by drying it in the sun.
Example from Ghana: Salt is produced at Ada and Elmina by allowing sea water to evaporate in shallow ponds under the hot sun. The salt left behind is then collected.

Think About It

How would you separate a mixture of sand and iron filings? Could you use a magnet? Why or why not?

Try This

Mix a handful of rice with three small stones. Try to separate them using picking. Then mix some dry sand with gari and try to separate them using a sieve. Record which method worked faster.

Unit Summary

A mixture contains two or more substances that are not joined together. Mixtures can be separated by picking, sieving, filtering or evaporation, depending on the size and type of the parts.

Unit 4 · Changing States of Matter

Sub-strand: Materials
B3.1.2.3.1

Learning Objectives

  • Name the three states of matter: solid, liquid and gas.
  • Describe how a substance changes from one state to another.
  • Give examples of melting, freezing, evaporation and condensation.

Key Words

Solid
Has a fixed shape and fixed volume (e.g. ice, stone).
Liquid
Has a fixed volume but takes the shape of its container (e.g. water).
Gas
Has no fixed shape or volume (e.g. water vapour).
Melting
Solid changing to liquid.
Freezing
Liquid changing to solid.
Condensation
Gas changing to liquid.

4.1 The Three States of Matter

Everything around you is in one of three states — solid, liquid or gas. Water is the best example because you can see all three states of water in Ghana:

  • Ice (solid): seen in freezers, or when you buy ice kenkey on a hot day.
  • Water (liquid): in rivers, wells and taps.
  • Steam (gas): rises from a boiling pot or from a kettle.

4.2 How Matter Changes State

  • Heating a solid turns it into a liquid → melting.
  • Cooling a liquid turns it into a solid → freezing.
  • Heating a liquid turns it into a gas → evaporation.
  • Cooling a gas turns it into a liquid → condensation.
Example from Ghana: During harmattan, wet clothes dry very quickly because the dry air helps water evaporate fast. In the rainy season, drying takes longer because the air is already full of water vapour.

Think About It

Why does a cold bottle of water become wet on the outside on a hot day? Where does the water come from?

Try This

Put three ice cubes in a cup and leave them in the sun. Observe what happens every five minutes. Write down what you see and explain which change of state took place.

Unit Summary

Matter exists as solid, liquid or gas. Heating or cooling causes a change of state: melting, freezing, evaporation and condensation. Water is the commonest example of a substance that changes state easily.

Unit 5 · Cyclic Events: Day, Night and Seasons

Sub-strand: Earth Science
B3.2.1.1.1

Learning Objectives

  • Explain why we have day and night.
  • Describe the two main seasons in Ghana and their effects.
  • Give examples of activities that depend on daily and seasonal cycles.

Key Words

Cycle
Something that repeats in the same order again and again.
Rotate
To turn around like a spinning top.
Day
The time when the sun is shining and it is bright.
Night
The time when it is dark and the moon and stars appear.
Harmattan
Dry, dusty wind that blows across Ghana in the dry season.

5.1 Day and Night

Every day the sun appears in the morning, moves across the sky, and disappears in the evening. Then night comes, and in the morning the sun rises again. This happens because the Earth rotates — it spins around like a top. The side of the Earth that faces the sun has day; the side turned away has night.

5.2 Seasons in Ghana

Ghana has two main seasons:

  • Rainy season — usually from April to October. Farmers plant maize, cassava, yam and cocoa. Rivers and streams fill up.
  • Dry season (harmattan) — usually from November to March. The air is dry and dusty, especially in the north. Grass dries up and bush fires can spread easily.
Example from Ghana: Farmers in the Ashanti Region plant cocoa at the beginning of the rainy season so the young seedlings get enough water to grow.

Think About It

If the Earth did not rotate, would we still have day and night? Explain what would happen.

Try This

Keep a weather diary for one week. Each day write: (i) what time the sun rises, (ii) what time it sets, (iii) whether it rained or was dry.

Unit Summary

The Earth rotates once every 24 hours, giving us day and night. Ghana has a rainy season and a dry harmattan season. Many of our daily and farming activities follow these cycles.

Unit 7 · The Water Cycle

Sub-strand: Earth Science
B3.2.1.3.1

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the four stages of the water cycle.
  • Explain how rain is formed.
  • Recognise the water cycle in everyday examples in Ghana.

Key Words

Water cycle
The continuous movement of water between the earth and the sky.
Evaporation
Water changing into vapour.
Condensation
Vapour changing back into tiny water drops.
Precipitation
Rain, hail or snow falling from clouds.
Collection
Water gathering in rivers, lakes and the sea.

7.1 The Four Stages

Water on earth is always moving in a cycle. The four main stages are:

  1. Evaporation — the sun heats water in rivers, lakes and the sea. The water turns into vapour and rises into the air.
  2. Condensation — high in the sky, the vapour cools and forms tiny water drops. These drops join together to form clouds.
  3. Precipitation — when the drops become heavy, they fall as rain.
  4. Collection — the rain water collects in rivers, lakes and the sea, and the cycle starts again.
Example from Ghana: Water from Lake Volta evaporates under the hot sun. It rises, condenses into clouds, and later falls as rain on the Volta Region, feeding the lake again.

Think About It

Try This

Half fill a clear bottle with warm water, then place a cold lid on top. Watch the underside of the lid. What do you see? This is condensation.

Unit Summary

The water cycle moves water through four stages — evaporation, condensation, precipitation and collection. This cycle brings rain and keeps our rivers and lakes filled.

Unit 8 · Water and Air as Natural Resources

Sub-strand: Earth Science
B3.2.1.4.1 · B3.2.1.4.2

Learning Objectives

  • State the importance of water and air to living things.
  • List simple ways of keeping water and air clean.
  • Describe what happens when water or air becomes polluted.

Key Words

Natural resource
Something found in nature that living things use.
Pollution
Making water, air or land dirty and unsafe.
Conserve
To protect and use something carefully so it lasts.
Clean
Free from dirt or germs.

8.1 Water — A Precious Resource

We use water for drinking, cooking, bathing, washing, farming and for animals. Without water, no living thing can survive for more than a few days. Even though about two-thirds of the Earth is covered by water, only a very small part of it is fresh water that we can drink.

8.2 Air — The Breath of Life

Air is all around us, even though we cannot see it. All animals and plants need air to live. Humans and animals breathe in the oxygen in air and breathe out carbon dioxide. Plants do the opposite — they take in carbon dioxide and give out oxygen. This keeps the air balanced.

8.3 Keeping Water and Air Clean

  • Do not throw rubbish into rivers, streams or gutters.
  • Do not defecate near water sources.
  • Boil or filter drinking water if it is not from a safe source.
  • Plant trees — they clean the air.
  • Avoid burning rubbish in the open — the smoke pollutes the air.
  • Use clean cooking fuels instead of charcoal where possible.
Example from Ghana: Galamsey (small-scale illegal mining) pollutes rivers such as the Pra and the Ankobra with mercury and mud. This makes it hard for communities to get clean drinking water and harms the fish in the rivers.

Think About It

Try This

Draw two pictures: one showing a clean river with fish and plants, and one showing a polluted river. Write two sentences about each picture.

Unit Summary

Water and air are natural resources that all living things need. We must keep them clean by not polluting rivers and by protecting the air we breathe. Pollution harms plants, animals and people.

Unit 9 · Life Cycle of a Plant

Sub-strand: Life Cycles of Organisms
B3.2.2.1

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the stages in the life cycle of a flowering plant.
  • Identify the conditions a seed needs to germinate.
  • Give examples from common Ghanaian crops.

Key Words

Life cycle
The stages a living thing passes through from birth to when it produces young ones.
Seed
The part of a plant from which a new plant grows.
Germination
When a seed begins to grow into a seedling.
Seedling
A young plant that has just grown from a seed.
Pollination
When pollen is carried from one flower to another.

9.1 The Stages

All flowering plants follow the same basic life cycle:

  1. Seed — the resting stage, containing a tiny plant inside.
  2. Germination — the seed takes in water and the tiny plant begins to grow.
  3. Seedling — a young green shoot with small leaves appears.
  4. Adult plant — the plant grows bigger with more leaves, roots and a stem.
  5. Flowering — the plant produces flowers.
  6. Fruit and seeds — after pollination, flowers form fruit containing new seeds.

9.2 What a Seed Needs to Germinate

  • Water — to soften the seed coat.
  • Air — the seed needs oxygen to grow.
  • Warmth — most seeds germinate faster in warm conditions.

Seeds do not need light to germinate, but once the seedling appears, it needs sunlight to make its own food.

Example from Ghana: A maize farmer in Techiman plants maize at the start of the rainy season. Within about a week the seeds germinate. In three months the maize is ready for harvest, giving new seeds for the next season.

Think About It

Why do you think some seeds can remain in the ground for many months before they germinate?

Try This

Place five maize seeds on wet cotton wool in a clear cup near a window. Check daily and record when the first shoot appears. Water it lightly each day.

Unit Summary

Flowering plants go through a life cycle: seed, germination, seedling, adult plant, flower, fruit and new seeds. Seeds need water, air and warmth to germinate.

Learning Objectives

  • Name the main parts of the human body.
  • Identify the five sense organs and their functions.
  • List simple habits that keep the body healthy.

Key Words

Sense organ
A part of the body that helps us detect the world around us.
Head
The top part of the body containing the brain and sense organs.
Trunk
The middle part of the body — the chest and abdomen.
Limbs
The arms and legs.
Hygiene
Habits that keep the body clean and healthy.

10.1 Main Parts of the Body

The human body is divided into three main parts:

  • Head — contains the brain, eyes, ears, nose, tongue and mouth.
  • Trunk — contains the chest (with the heart and lungs) and the abdomen (with the stomach and intestines).
  • Limbs — the arms and legs, which help us move and do work.

10.2 The Five Sense Organs

  • Eyes — for seeing.
  • Ears — for hearing.
  • Nose — for smelling.
  • Tongue — for tasting.
  • Skin — for feeling (touch, heat, cold and pain).

10.3 Keeping the Body Healthy

  • Bathe at least once a day.
  • Brush your teeth twice a day — morning and before bed.
  • Wash your hands with soap before eating and after using the toilet.
  • Eat a balanced diet with fruits and vegetables.
  • Drink clean water and get enough rest.
  • Exercise every day by running, playing or walking.
Example from Ghana: Washing your hands with soap under running water after using the toilet can prevent diseases like cholera and diarrhoea, which are common during the rainy season.

Think About It

Unit Summary

The human body has three main parts — head, trunk and limbs. We have five sense organs: eyes, ears, nose, tongue and skin. Good hygiene keeps the body healthy and prevents disease.

Learning Objectives

Key Words

Sun
A star — a ball of hot glowing gas that gives out light and heat.
Earth
The planet we live on — it goes around the sun.
Moon
A natural satellite that goes around the Earth.
Orbit
The path one object takes around another in space.

11.1 Three Bodies in Space

The sun, the Earth and the moon are three of the most important objects for us.

  • The sun is a star. It is very hot and gives out its own light.
  • The Earth is a planet. It moves around the sun and takes about 365 days to make one complete orbit.
  • The moon is a natural satellite. It moves around the Earth and takes about 28 days to do so.

11.2 Light from the Moon

The moon does not produce its own light. We can see it because it reflects light from the sun, just like a mirror. As the moon orbits the Earth, we see different parts of its lit side. These are called the phases of the moon.

Example from Ghana: Fishermen along the coast of Ghana often plan their fishing trips around the phases of the moon. On full-moon nights, they can see well; on new-moon nights, it is darker.

Unit Summary

The sun is a star, the Earth is a planet that orbits the sun, and the moon is a satellite that orbits the Earth. The moon reflects sunlight — it does not make its own light.

Unit 12 · Organisms and Their Habitats

Sub-strand: Ecosystems
B3.3.3.1.1

Learning Objectives

  • Explain what a habitat is.
  • Name the main habitats in Ghana and give examples of organisms living there.
  • Describe how organisms are suited to their habitats.

Key Words

Habitat
The natural home of a plant or an animal.
Organism
Any living thing — plant, animal or microbe.
Ecosystem
A community of living things and their surroundings.
Adapt
To have features that help an organism survive in its habitat.

12.1 What is a Habitat?

A habitat is the natural home of a living thing. It provides food, water, shelter and space. Different organisms live in different habitats because each habitat has its own conditions — such as how wet, dry, hot or cold it is.

12.2 Habitats in Ghana

  • Forest — tall trees, plenty of rain. Examples of organisms: monkeys, parrots, cocoa trees, ferns.
  • Savanna — grassland with scattered trees, drier conditions. Examples: antelopes, guinea fowl, baobab trees, shea trees.
  • Rivers and ponds — fresh water. Examples: tilapia, water lilies, dragonflies, frogs.
  • Sea — salty water. Examples: sardines, crabs, seaweed.

12.3 Adaptation to Habitats

Living things have features that help them survive where they live. This is called adaptation. For example, a baobab tree stores water in its thick trunk to survive the dry savanna. A tilapia has fins and gills that help it swim and breathe in water. A monkey has long arms and a tail for climbing trees in the forest.

Example from Ghana: The mole cricket lives underground and digs tunnels with its strong front legs. Its body colour matches the soil, helping it hide from birds that eat it.

Think About It

What would happen to a fish if you took it out of water? What does this tell you about its habitat?

Try This

Choose one habitat near your school (forest, garden or pond). List three plants and three animals that live there.

Unit Summary

A habitat is the natural home of living things. Ghana has several habitats including forests, savannas, rivers and the sea. Organisms have adaptations that help them survive in their habitats.

Unit 13 · Energy Around Us

Sub-strand: Sources and Forms of Energy
B3.4.1.1.1

Learning Objectives

  • Name the main forms of energy we see around us.
  • Identify sources of energy at home and in school.
  • Explain why energy is important in daily life.

Key Words

Energy
The ability to do work or make things happen.
Source
Where something comes from.
Light
A form of energy that lets us see.
Heat
A form of energy that makes things warm.
Sound
A form of energy we can hear.

13.1 Forms of Energy

Energy is everywhere. We can see and feel its effects every day:

  • Light energy — from the sun, torches, candles, bulbs.
  • Heat energy — from fire, the sun, a boiling pot.
  • Sound energy — from drums, radio, people talking.
  • Movement (kinetic) energy — from a moving car, a running child, wind.

13.2 Sources of Energy

The main sources of energy we use in Ghana include:

  • The sun — gives light and heat.
  • Firewood and charcoal — used for cooking.
  • Electricity — from the Akosombo Dam and other power stations.
  • Food — gives our bodies the energy to work and play.
Example from Ghana: The Akosombo Dam on the Volta River uses the energy of falling water to turn big machines called turbines. These turbines generate electricity that lights homes, schools and hospitals across the country.

Unit 14 · Simple Electric Circuits

Sub-strand: Electricity and Electronics
B3.4.2.1.1

Learning Objectives

  • Identify the parts of a simple electric circuit.
  • Explain the difference between conductors and insulators.
  • State simple rules for using electricity safely.

Key Words

Circuit
A complete path that electricity flows through.
Battery
A store of electric energy.
Bulb
A lamp that lights up when electricity passes through it.
Switch
A device that opens or closes a circuit.
Conductor
A material that lets electricity pass through it.
Insulator
A material that does not let electricity pass through it.

14.1 Parts of a Simple Circuit

A simple electric circuit needs three main parts:

  • A battery — to supply the electricity.
  • Wires — to carry the electricity.
  • A bulb (or buzzer) — to show that electricity is flowing.

A switch can be added to turn the circuit on and off. When the switch is closed, the circuit is complete and the bulb lights up. When the switch is open, the circuit is broken and the bulb goes off.

14.2 Conductors and Insulators

  • Conductors let electricity pass through them: metals like copper, iron and aluminium; also water and the human body.
  • Insulators do not let electricity pass through them: rubber, plastic, wood, dry cloth and glass.

Wires are made of copper (a conductor) covered with plastic (an insulator) for safety.

14.3 Using Electricity Safely

  • Never touch electric sockets with wet hands.
  • Do not put metal objects into sockets.
  • Do not play near broken electric poles or fallen wires.
  • Ask an adult to fix faulty wires or sockets.
Example from Ghana: Many homes use rechargeable lamps charged from the mains or solar panels. The lamp has a battery inside — when it is turned on, the circuit is closed and the bulb lights up.

Think About It

Why is the outside of an electric wire covered with plastic and not with copper?

Try This

Unit Summary

A simple circuit has a battery, wires and a bulb, with a switch to control it. Conductors let electricity pass through; insulators do not. Electricity must be used safely.

Unit 15 · Pushes and Pulls

Sub-strand: Forces and Movement
B3.4.3.1.1

Learning Objectives

  • Explain what a force is.
  • Give examples of pushes and pulls in daily life.
  • Describe how friction affects movement.

Key Words

Force
A push or a pull on an object.
Push
A force that moves an object away from you.
Pull
A force that moves an object towards you.
Friction
A force that slows things down when they rub against each other.

15.1 What is a Force?

A force is a push or a pull that acts on an object. Forces can:

  • Make a still object move — e.g. kicking a ball.
  • Make a moving object stop — e.g. catching a ball.
  • Change the direction of a moving object — e.g. bouncing a ball.
  • Change the shape of an object — e.g. squeezing a tomato.

15.2 Pushes and Pulls Around Us

  • Push — pushing a wheelbarrow, opening a door away from you, kicking a football.
  • Pull — pulling a bucket from a well, dragging a bag, opening a door towards you.

15.3 Friction

Friction is the force that slows things down when two surfaces rub against each other. Friction is useful — it helps our shoes grip the ground so we don't slip. It also helps bicycle brakes work. But sometimes friction is a problem — it wears out the soles of our shoes and makes machines hot.

Example from Ghana: When a trotro's tyres are worn smooth, they cannot grip the road well, especially in the rain. That is why drivers must change old tyres — friction keeps the vehicle safe.

Think About It

Why is it harder to walk on a slippery floor than on a rough one? Use the word friction in your answer.

Try This

Push a small toy car across a smooth table, then across a rough mat. Which surface slows the car more? This shows the effect of friction.

Unit Summary

A force is a push or a pull. Forces can make objects start, stop, change direction or change shape. Friction is a force that slows movement when surfaces rub together.
